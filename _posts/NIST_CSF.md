## NIST CSF 

### Why
[cite_start]The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 provides guidance to industry, government agencies, and other organizations for managing cybersecurity risks[cite: 1]. [cite_start]It was created because cybersecurity risks are constantly expanding, and managing them must be a continuous process for all organizations, regardless of their sophistication[cite: 37, 38]. [cite_start]The framework helps organizations better understand, assess, prioritize, and communicate their cybersecurity efforts[cite: 2, 53].

### What
[cite_start]It is a foundational resource that offers a taxonomy of high-level cybersecurity outcomes[cite: 2, 58]. [cite_start]The framework is not a prescriptive checklist of actions; rather, it links to online resources that provide guidance on practices and controls to achieve those outcomes[cite: 3, 4]. [cite_start]Its main components are the **CSF Core**, **CSF Organizational Profiles**, and **CSF Tiers**[cite: 41].

### Where
[cite_start]The CSF can be used by any organization, regardless of its size, sector, or maturity, including industry, government, academia, and nonprofits[cite: 2, 20]. [cite_start]Its principles and referenced standards are not country-specific and have been successfully used by organizations inside and outside the United States[cite: 59]. [cite_start]It applies to all information and communications technology (ICT), including information technology (IT), the Internet of Things (IoT), operational technology (OT), cloud, and mobile environments[cite: 104, 105].

### Who
[cite_start]The primary audience includes individuals responsible for developing and leading cybersecurity programs[cite: 7]. [cite_start]It is also intended for others involved in managing risk, such as executives, boards of directors, technology professionals, risk managers, and auditors[cite: 8]. [cite_start]Additionally, it is useful for policymakers and professional organizations that set priorities for cybersecurity risk management[cite: 9].

### When
[cite_start]Version 2.0 of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework was published on **February 26, 2024**[cite: 1]. [cite_start]It builds upon previous versions of the framework, which was formerly titled the “Framework for Improving Critical Infrastructure Cybersecurity”[cite: 14, 33]. [cite_start]The CSF is designed to be a long-term guide, as managing cybersecurity risk is a continuous, ongoing process[cite: 37, 39].

### How
[cite_start]Organizations use the framework to understand, assess, prioritize, and communicate cybersecurity risks[cite: 53]. [cite_start]A key method involves creating **Organizational Profiles** that describe the organization's current and target cybersecurity postures, allowing them to analyze gaps and create a prioritized action plan[cite: 45, 128, 129]. [cite_start]The framework provides a common language to improve risk management communication between executives, managers, and practitioners[cite: 56, 170, 171].

## Decoding the NIST CSF 2.0

[cite_start]The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 provides a flexible and comprehensive approach to managing cybersecurity risk for organizations of all types[cite: 20]. [cite_start]It is not a one-size-fits-all solution but a voluntary guide that can be tailored to an organization's specific mission, risks, and objectives[cite: 22, 63]. [cite_start]The framework's goal is to enable better communication and management of cybersecurity risk across all levels of an organization[cite: 56].

---

## Core Components

[cite_start]The framework is built on three main parts that work together to create a holistic cybersecurity risk management program[cite: 41].

### 1. The CSF Core
[cite_start]The **Core** is the nucleus of the framework, providing a taxonomy of high-level cybersecurity outcomes[cite: 42]. [cite_start]It is organized into a hierarchy of Functions, Categories, and Subcategories[cite: 42]. The six core **Functions** are:
* [cite_start]**GOVERN (GV)**: A new and central function in CSF 2.0, it establishes the organization’s cybersecurity risk management strategy, expectations, and policies[cite: 74]. [cite_start]It is critical for integrating cybersecurity into the organization's broader enterprise risk management (ERM) strategy[cite: 76].
* [cite_start]**IDENTIFY (ID)**: Understanding the organization's assets, suppliers, and related cybersecurity risks to prioritize efforts[cite: 78, 79].
* [cite_start]**PROTECT (PR)**: Using safeguards to secure assets and manage cybersecurity risks[cite: 81, 82].
* [cite_start]**DETECT (DE)**: Finding and analyzing potential cybersecurity attacks and other adverse events[cite: 85, 86].
* [cite_start]**RESPOND (RS)**: Taking action to contain the effects of a detected cybersecurity incident[cite: 87, 88].
* [cite_start]**RECOVER (RC)**: Restoring assets and operations affected by a cybersecurity incident[cite: 89, 90].



### 2. CSF Organizational Profiles
[cite_start]**Profiles** are used to describe an organization's current and/or target cybersecurity posture using the outcomes from the CSF Core[cite: 45, 108].
* [cite_start]A **Current Profile** specifies the outcomes an organization is currently achieving[cite: 111].
* [cite_start]A **Target Profile** specifies the desired outcomes an organization wants to achieve based on its risk management objectives[cite: 112].

[cite_start]By comparing the Current and Target Profiles, an organization can identify gaps and develop a prioritized action plan to improve its cybersecurity posture[cite: 128, 129].

### 3. CSF Tiers
[cite_start]**Tiers** characterize the rigor of an organization’s cybersecurity risk governance and management practices[cite: 46, 137]. [cite_start]They help provide context on how an organization views and manages risk[cite: 47]. The four Tiers are:
* [cite_start]**Tier 1: Partial** (ad hoc and reactive) [cite: 138, 250]
* [cite_start]**Tier 2: Risk Informed** (management-approved practices, but not organization-wide) [cite: 138, 255]
* [cite_start]**Tier 3: Repeatable** (formally approved policies and procedures are in place) [cite: 138, 262]
* [cite_start]**Tier 4: Adaptive** (practices are continuously improved based on lessons learned and predictive indicators) [cite: 138, 280]

## Using the Framework for Continuous Improvement

[cite_start]The CSF provides a simple, repeatable process for enhancing cybersecurity risk management[cite: 133]. An organization can:
1.  [cite_start]**Scope the Profile**: Define what part of the organization the profile will cover (e.g., the entire enterprise, a specific system)[cite: 119, 120].
2.  [cite_start]**Gather Information**: Collect relevant data like organizational policies, risk management priorities, and known requirements[cite: 123, 124].
3.  [cite_start]**Create the Profile**: Document the Current Profile and define a Target Profile based on risks and objectives[cite: 125, 126].
4.  [cite_start]**Analyze Gaps & Plan**: Compare the two profiles to identify gaps and create a prioritized action plan to address them[cite: 128, 129].
5.  [cite_start]**Implement & Update**: Execute the action plan to move toward the Target Profile and repeat the process as needed for continual improvement[cite: 130, 131, 133].

## A Foundation for Better Risk Management

The CSF enhances risk management by improving communication and integration. [cite_start]It fosters a bidirectional flow of information between executives, managers, and practitioners, ensuring that cybersecurity efforts are aligned with organizational priorities[cite: 170, 171]. It is also designed to integrate with other risk management programs, such as:
* [cite_start]**Enterprise Risk Management (ERM)** [cite: 181]
* [cite_start]**Privacy Risk Management** (using the NIST Privacy Framework) [cite: 204]
* [cite_start]**Cybersecurity Supply Chain Risk Management (C-SCRM)** [cite: 211]
* [cite_start]**Risks from Emerging Technologies** like Artificial Intelligence (using the NIST AI RMF) [cite: 214, 216]
